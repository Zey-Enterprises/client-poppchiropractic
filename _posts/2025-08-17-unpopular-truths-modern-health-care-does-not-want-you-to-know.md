<p id="viewer-gp52j33372"><strong><em>Do screenings extend life expectancy? </em></strong></p>
<p id="viewer-hl2ks414"><strong><em>Everyone thinks they do, but in reality most of the common ones do not..</em></strong></p>
<h2 id="viewer-ttdpg418"><strong>1: Mammograms</strong></h2>
<p id="viewer-7htky421">Routine mammogram screening is widely used for early detection of breast cancer, but its impact on overall life expectancy is limited or marginal for most women, especially when considering the general population.</p>
<h3 id="viewer-vkq30424">Minimal or No Increase in Life Expectancy</h3>
<ul>
<li id="viewer-wgxdw426">Recent systematic analysis found that mammography screening did <strong>not significantly increase overall lifespan</strong> in the screened group compared to those who were not screened, with a mean gain of 0 days (95% CI, −190 to 237 days). This suggests no statistically significant longevity benefit for the general population from mammography screening{% include citation.html scope="mammograms" label="1" %}{% include citation.html scope="mammograms" label="2" %}.</li>
<li id="viewer-za9l0430">Other studies showed that for women continuing screening after age 69, the added average life expectancy was extremely small—about <strong>2.1 days to 7.2 hours</strong>, even when large cohorts were studied{% include citation.html scope="mammograms" label="3" %}{% include citation.html scope="mammograms" label="4" %}. </li>
</ul>

<h3 id="viewer-yhq2m435">Cancer-Specific Mortality Reduction</h3>
<p id="viewer-ci33s437"> Mammograms do reduce the <strong>risk of dying from breast cancer</strong>, with estimates ranging from about a 15–25% decrease in breast cancer-specific deaths, particularly for women regularly screened between <strong>ages 40–74</strong>{% include citation.html scope="mammograms" label="5" %}{% include citation.html scope="mammograms" label="6" %}{% include citation.html scope="mammograms" label="7" %}.</p>
<p id="viewer-30bdq443"> However, since breast cancer represents a small proportion of overall deaths in most age groups, the effect on total life expectancy across populations is very slight.</p>
<h3 id="viewer-w3j7c446">Population-Level Impact</h3>
<p id="viewer-qmlzt448">  The majority of women undergoing mammography screening will <strong>not have their lives extended by the intervention</strong>, due to the relatively low incidence of fatal breast cancer in the general population and the fact that most women will die from other causes{% include citation.html scope="mammograms" label="8" %}{% include citation.html scope="mammograms" label="2" %}.</p>
<p id="viewer-kt6gn452">   For certain sub-populations (such as those with strong risk factors or longer life expectancy), the gain may be slightly more, but on average, the impact remains marginal.</p>
<h3 id="viewer-gabkm455">Considerations</h3>
<h3 id="viewer-1ugj7458">Harms and Trade-offs</h3>
<p id="viewer-3oe0f460">   Routine screening does carry risks—false positives, unnecessary biopsies, anxiety, and possible overtreatment—which must be balanced against the marginal gain in all-cause life expectancy{% include citation.html scope="mammograms" label="8" %}.</p>
<ul><li id="viewer-kx5z8462">Guidelines stress shared decision-making, especially for women over 75 or with limited life expectancy, as the benefits diminish further and harms may increase with age and comorbidities{% include citation.html scope="mammograms" label="9" %}{% include citation.html scope="mammograms" label="10" %}.</li></ul>

<h3 id="viewer-giq8n481">
<strong>Conclusion:</strong>  </h3>
<p id="viewer-mjz16484">Routine mammogram screening may slightly reduce deaths from breast cancer but does not meaningfully alter total life expectancy for most women. The decision to screen should be individualized, weighing the small potential benefit in lifespan against possible harms and each woman's personal values and health situation.</p>

<h2 id="viewer-0nhef571"><strong>2: Prostate Screenings</strong></h2>
<p id="viewer-9eg1w574">Routine prostate cancer screening (primarily with the prostate-specific antigen or PSA test) has only a very small effect, if any, on overall life expectancy in the general population. Here are the key findings:</p>
<h3 id="viewer-8h7lo577">Effect on Life Expectancy</h3>
<p id="viewer-o9gg0579">Large, long-term studies and meta-analyses show that routine PSA screening <strong>does not produce a statistically significant increase in overall lifespan</strong> for most men{% include citation.html scope="prostate-screenings" label="1" %}{% include citation.html scope="prostate-screenings" label="2" %}.</p>
<p id="viewer-4wm7e583"> A recent systematic review found that prostate cancer screening with PSA may extend life by about <strong>37 days (around 5 weeks)</strong>, with the true effect potentially ranging from no benefit to a bit more, but this increase was <strong>not statistically significant</strong>{% include citation.html scope="prostate-screenings" label="1" %}.</p>
<p id="viewer-xyfur589"> Major randomized trials (like PLCO and ERSPC) have shown <strong>no reduction in all-cause mortality</strong> from PSA screening, even though some have found a small decrease in prostate cancer–specific deaths{% include citation.html scope="prostate-screenings" label="2" %}.</p>
<h3 id="viewer-qhx7m594">Cancer-Specific Outcomes</h3>
<p id="viewer-xxtj0596">PSA screening can <strong>reduce the risk of dying from prostate cancer</strong> itself by about 20% in some studies{% include citation.html scope="prostate-screenings" label="3" %}{% include citation.html scope="prostate-screenings" label="2" %}, but since deaths from prostate cancer are relatively uncommon in the general male population, this <strong>does not translate into a meaningful gain in total life expectancy</strong>.</p>
<p id="viewer-edj9q602">To prevent a single death from prostate cancer, a large number of men must be screened—estimates suggest about 781 men screened to prevent one prostate cancer death over 13 years{% include citation.html scope="prostate-screenings" label="2" %}{% include citation.html scope="prostate-screenings" label="4" %}.</p>
<h3 id="viewer-e79d2605">Potential Harms</h3>
<p id="viewer-faxtd607">Routine PSA screening leads to:</p>
<ul>
<li id="viewer-hnj6u609">Many false positives and unnecessary prostate biopsies.</li>
<li id="viewer-w8hzm611">Overdiagnosis: detection of slow-growing cancers that would never have caused harm in a man's lifetime.</li>
<li id="viewer-n3hno613">Overtreatment, which can result in urinary incontinence, erectile dysfunction, and bowel problems{% include citation.html scope="prostate-screenings" label="5" %}{% include citation.html scope="prostate-screenings" label="6" %}{% include citation.html scope="prostate-screenings" label="4" %}{% include citation.html scope="prostate-screenings" label="7" %}.</li>
<li id="viewer-jghvl615">Because so many men with detected prostate cancer would die of other causes or never experience symptoms, many are <strong>treated unnecessarily</strong>, experiencing side effects without longevity benefits{% include citation.html scope="prostate-screenings" label="4" %}.</li>
</ul>



<h3 id="viewer-xjr5a620"><strong>Guideline Recommendations</strong></h3>
<ul>
<li id="viewer-r0d8a622">Most health guidelines recommend that men <strong>make individualized, informed decisions</strong> about PSA screening, especially considering age and personal risk.</li>
<li id="viewer-vngj2626">Screening is generally <strong>not recommended for men with a life expectancy of less than 10–15 years</strong> or for those over 70 in average health{% include citation.html scope="prostate-screenings" label="8" %}{% include citation.html scope="prostate-screenings" label="9" %}{% include citation.html scope="prostate-screenings" label="10" %}.</li>
</ul>

<h3 id="viewer-6xkii647">
<strong>Conclusion:</strong>  </h3>
<p id="viewer-gktfq650">Routine PSA-based prostate cancer screening produces, at best, a <strong>very slight or no increase in total life expectancy</strong> for most men. The reduction in deaths from prostate cancer is outweighed by minimal impact on overall lifespan and significant risks of overdiagnosis and overtreatment. Decisions about screening should be based on individual preferences, risk factors, and in discussion with a healthcare provider.</p>

<h2 id="viewer-nxg2e742"><strong>3: Colonoscopy</strong></h2>
<p id="viewer-e0rjk745">Routine colonoscopy and sigmoidoscopy are both used for colorectal cancer (CRC) screening, but they differ in their impact on overall life expectancy when used in population-widescreening. Evidence shows that <strong>routine colonoscopies add little to overall life expectancy, while routine sigmoidoscopies convey a modest but statistically significant benefit</strong>.</p>
<h3 id="viewer-nasjt750">Routine Colonoscopy Screening</h3>
<h3 id="viewer-yyoga753">Very Modest Impact on Life Expectancy</h3>
<ul>
<li id="viewer-3092g755">Multiple analyses report that routine colonoscopy screening extends life by <strong>about 37–170 days</strong> (roughly 1–6 months), with some estimates closer to 37 days, and confidence intervals often spanning from potential harm to modest benefit. These changes are <strong>not always statistically significant</strong>{% include citation.html scope="colonoscopy" label="1" %}{% include citation.html scope="colonoscopy" label="2" %}{% include citation.html scope="colonoscopy" label="3" %}.</li>
<li id="viewer-09a22761">For a typical adult population, this translates into an extremely small average gain: <strong>104–123 days per person screened</strong> from ages 45 to 75, according to large modeling studies{% include citation.html scope="colonoscopy" label="2" %}.</li>
<li id="viewer-iiudo765">Studies emphasize that many people screened will not see any extension in their lifespan because colorectal cancer is only one of many possible causes of death.</li>
</ul>


<h3 id="viewer-7c4mv768"><strong>Benefits and Harms</strong></h3>
<ul>
<li id="viewer-dpo9l771">Colonoscopies detect and remove pre-cancerous polyps, reducing CRC incidence and deaths. However, since overall mortality impact is so minimal, routine use in unselected populations, especially those with limited life expectancy or significant comorbidities, has <strong>limited net value</strong>{% include citation.html scope="colonoscopy" label="4" %}{% include citation.html scope="colonoscopy" label="5" %}.</li>
<li id="viewer-kfgt7775">Procedure risks (e.g., perforation, bleeding), discomfort, and preparation burden must also be considered.</li>
</ul>

<h3 id="viewer-2pn0x778">Routine Sigmoidoscopy Screening</h3>
<h3 id="viewer-98m53781"><strong>Demonstrated Life Expectancy Benefit</strong></h3>
<ul>
<li id="viewer-io6yj784">
<strong>Long-term randomized trials and recent meta-analyses consistently show that sigmoidoscopy as a routine screening test can extend life expectancy by about 110 days (approximately 3–4 months)</strong>—a statistically significant and clinically meaningful gain compared to colonoscopy and other major cancer screening strategies{% include citation.html scope="colonoscopy" label="1" %}{% include citation.html scope="colonoscopy" label="6" %}{% include citation.html scope="colonoscopy" label="7" %}{% include citation.html scope="colonoscopy" label="8" %}{% include citation.html scope="colonoscopy" label="9" %}.</li>
<li id="viewer-igqy9788">The benefit appears after a lag of 5–10 years and is greatest in persons with a <strong>life expectancy &gt;10 years</strong>{% include citation.html scope="colonoscopy" label="10" %}.</li>
<li id="viewer-63y1t792">Sigmoidoscopy reduces both the incidence of and deaths from colorectal cancer—particularly cancers located in the distal colon and rectum, which are accessible by the scope{% include citation.html scope="colonoscopy" label="11" %}{% include citation.html scope="colonoscopy" label="12" %}{% include citation.html scope="colonoscopy" label="13" %}.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="viewer-8ogt0905"><strong>4: Regular doctor visits and physicals</strong></h2>
<h3 id="viewer-1flh6908">The Impact of Routine Scheduled Doctor Visits (Physicals) on Life Expectancy</h3>
<h3 id="viewer-4tuz4911"> Key Findings</h3>
<ul>
<li id="viewer-t4udo914"><strong>Routine scheduled physicals do not increase overall life expectancy.</strong></li>
<li id="viewer-n0lzk917">Multiple high-quality randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses show that regularly scheduled, comprehensive health checks (general physicals for asymptomatic adults) do <strong>not reduce total mortality, cancer mortality, or cardiovascular mortality</strong> compared to the absence of such routine visits for the general population{% include citation.html scope="physicals" label="1" %}{% include citation.html scope="physicals" label="2" %}{% include citation.html scope="physicals" label="3" %}{% include citation.html scope="physicals" label="4" %}.</li>
<li id="viewer-u9dv0921">A Cochrane review of 17 trials (over 250,000 adults) found that invitations to general health checks had <strong>little or no effect on the risk of death from any cause (RR 1.00, 95% CI 0.97 to 1.03)</strong> and did not reduce deaths from cancer or cardiovascular causes{% include citation.html scope="physicals" label="1" %}{% include citation.html scope="physicals" label="3" %}. These findings are robust, with a high degree of certainty from large datasets.</li>
<li id="viewer-k5c3d925">While these visits often detect more new diagnoses and can increase the use of preventive services (vaccinations, cancer screenings), this <strong>does not translate into longer life on average for healthy adults</strong>{% include citation.html scope="physicals" label="2" %}.</li>
</ul>



<h3 id="viewer-1dchw930"><strong>Why don't routine physicals increase life expectancy?</strong></h3>
<ul>
<li id="viewer-1bymz933">Many serious illnesses develop between visits or can be detected and managed based on symptoms or risk factors during other medical consultations.</li>
<li id="viewer-gz014935">Primary care doctors are already likely to monitor relevant risks and intervene during visits for other concerns.</li>
<li id="viewer-1a0eh937">People at highest risk may avoid or not follow up on recommendations from routine visits.</li>
<li id="viewer-ayg7b939">Over-testing during physicals can lead to false positives, unnecessary further tests, anxiety, and potential overtreatment, while not significantly changing long-term outcomes{% include citation.html scope="physicals" label="5" %}{% include citation.html scope="physicals" label="6" %}{% include citation.html scope="physicals" label="4" %}{% include citation.html scope="physicals" label="2" %}.</li>
</ul>



<h3 id="viewer-9oo7l942"><strong>Potential Benefits of Routine Visits</strong></h3>
<ul>
<li id="viewer-pr3kp945">Routine check-ups <strong>do improve vaccination rates, chronic illness management (e.g., hypertension, depression)</strong>, and may increase patients' engagement in their own health{% include citation.html scope="physicals" label="7" %}{% include citation.html scope="physicals" label="8" %}. These are valuable, but measurable gains in life expectancy are not observed.</li>
<li id="viewer-yxgde949">They can also improve the doctor–patient relationship, provide reassurance, and create opportunities for early intervention on emerging health or lifestyle problems—yet these benefits do not equate to a significant reduction in deaths from major diseases{% include citation.html scope="physicals" label="1" %}{% include citation.html scope="physicals" label="7" %}{% include citation.html scope="physicals" label="2" %}{% include citation.html scope="physicals" label="9" %}.</li>
</ul>

<h3 id="viewer-wggx9952"><strong>Harms, Costs, and Considerations</strong></h3>
<ul>
<li id="viewer-yjd1f955">Unnecessary screening in healthy people creates the risk of overdiagnosis, excess healthcare spending, patient anxiety, and potential for harm from unnecessary procedures{% include citation.html scope="physicals" label="5" %}{% include citation.html scope="physicals" label="2" %}{% include citation.html scope="physicals" label="6" %}{% include citation.html scope="physicals" label="4" %}.</li>
<li id="viewer-hvc3f957">Frequent visits to healthcare facilities may even increase exposure to hospital-acquired illnesses, particularly in older adults{% include citation.html scope="physicals" label="10" %}.</li>
<li id="viewer-wmhni959">For healthy adults, many expert groups now advise <strong>against routine annual physicals</strong>, recommending instead targeted, risk-based preventive care{% include citation.html scope="physicals" label="5" %}{% include citation.html scope="physicals" label="2" %}{% include citation.html scope="physicals" label="1" %}.</li>
</ul>

<p id="viewer-9amzc987"><strong>Routine, scheduled doctor visits for physicals do not increase overall life expectancy for healthy, asymptomatic adults. <h2 id="viewer-5fyxp1070"><strong>5: Dermatology Screenings</strong></h2>
<p id="viewer-rxe721073"><strong>Routine dermatologist screenings have not been shown to increase overall life expectancy in the general population.</strong></p>
<p id="viewer-w44701075"> Large, well-controlled studies and systematic reviews confirm that, for most people, regularly scheduled full-body skin exams by a dermatologist do <strong>not measurably reduce all-cause mortality or extend lifespan</strong> when compared to people who do not attend such screenings{% include citation.html scope="dermatology-screenings" label="1" %}{% include citation.html scope="dermatology-screenings" label="2" %}{% include citation.html scope="dermatology-screenings" label="3" %}{% include citation.html scope="dermatology-screenings" label="4" %}{% include citation.html scope="dermatology-screenings" label="5" %}.</p>
<h3 id="viewer-v6t5p1080">Early Detection and Cancer-Specific Outcomes</h3>
<ul>
<li id="viewer-938fe1082">Skin checks by dermatologists can find skin cancers, including melanoma, at an earlier, thinner stage—which does improve cancer-specific outcomes such as survival from melanoma if detected early{% include citation.html scope="dermatology-screenings" label="3" %}{% include citation.html scope="dermatology-screenings" label="6" %}{% include citation.html scope="dermatology-screenings" label="7" %}.</li>
<li id="viewer-h60lz1084">However, the absolute reduction in deaths from melanoma is very small at a population level, mainly because:</li>
<li id="viewer-qcblj1086">Melanoma is rare relative to other causes of death, and</li>
<li id="viewer-jt23k1088">Most suspicious skin lesions found during screening are not dangerous, leading to excessive biopsies and overdiagnosis without meaningful gains in total life expectancy{% include citation.html scope="dermatology-screenings" label="8" %}{% include citation.html scope="dermatology-screenings" label="5" %}.</li>
</ul>



<h3 id="viewer-7hgy91091"><strong>Evidence from Population Studies and Reviews</strong></h3>
<ul>
<li id="viewer-fdmbf1094">A large systematic review of cancer screening found <strong>no statistically significant increase in overall longevity</strong>—in days or years—due to skin cancer screening in the general population{% include citation.html scope="dermatology-screenings" label="2" %}.</li>
<li id="viewer-fda6m1098">Cost-effectiveness models show only minimal quality-adjusted life-year (QALY) or survival gains for mass screening, even in higher-risk groups, and these gains are dwarfed by those of more effective screening programs (such as for colon cancer){% include citation.html scope="dermatology-screenings" label="1" %}.</li>
<li id="viewer-40kjc1100">A 2021 cohort study reported that routine skin checks were associated with lower all-cause mortality after adjusting for some (but not all) potential confounding factors, but <strong>no significant effect on melanoma-specific mortality remained after more rigorous adjustment for tumor characteristics and patient health—suggesting that healthier people, not the screening itself, may account for better survival rates observed in some studies</strong>{% include citation.html scope="dermatology-screenings" label="3" %}{% include citation.html scope="dermatology-screenings" label="4" %}.</li>
</ul>


<h3 id="viewer-36ejd1105"><strong>Risks and Harms of Routine Screenings</strong></h3>
<ul>
<li id="viewer-bqbn41108">Routine full-body skin exams can result in:</li>
<li id="viewer-9sdmu1110">Overdiagnosis and unnecessary removal of harmless lesions</li>
<li id="viewer-igj0s1112">Increased anxiety for patients (the "worried well" phenomenon)</li>
<li id="viewer-uh1er1114">Possible surgical complications from biopsies and excisions{% include citation.html scope="dermatology-screenings" label="8" %}{% include citation.html scope="dermatology-screenings" label="9" %}</li>
<li id="viewer-anucj1116">These downsides occur without a proven life expectancy benefit for most people.</li>
</ul>




<h3 id="viewer-kcnzu1119"><strong>Current Guidelines and Recommendations</strong></h3>
<ul><li id="viewer-5llbb1122">Major expert organizations such as the US Preventive Services Task Force have found insufficient evidence to recommend routine skin cancer screening for asymptomatic adults as a measure to improve overall survival or prolong life{% include citation.html scope="dermatology-screenings" label="5" %}.</li></ul>

<h3 id="viewer-63g7q1143">Conclusion</h3>
<p id="viewer-v9e7i1146"><strong>Routine, scheduled skin checks by dermatologists do not increase overall life expectancy for the general adult population.</strong> While these exams may catch some cancers early—improving individual cancer survival odds—this does not translate into a meaningful increase in how long people live. For most adults, routine visits should be replaced with targeted exams for those at high risk or those with suspicious lesions. Overuse of routine skin screening may yield more harm than benefit in otherwise healthy individuals{% include citation.html scope="dermatology-screenings" label="8" %}{% include citation.html scope="dermatology-screenings" label="1" %}{% include citation.html scope="dermatology-screenings" label="2" %}{% include citation.html scope="dermatology-screenings" label="3" %}{% include citation.html scope="dermatology-screenings" label="4" %}{% include citation.html scope="dermatology-screenings" label="5" %}.</p>
